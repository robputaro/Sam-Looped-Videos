# Preloaded Episode Library

## The Operator Call

14 prepared cases. Each includes the scenario hook, three evidence points, three choices, Sam's recommended first move, the reasoning, an operator rule, and an optional Looped bridge.

### 01. More leads — or fix the handoff?
**Category:** Acquisition
**Hook:** The clinic wants to increase ad spend next month. Would you?
**Operator call:** Fix the conversion system before buying more traffic.
**Rule:** Traffic is a multiplier — not a repair strategy.
**Why it is useful:** A classic “marketing problem” that is actually a conversion-system problem.

### 02. Busy now. Empty later?
**Category:** Retention
**Hook:** The schedule is full this month. Rebooking is weak. What would you do next?
**Operator call:** Protect future demand before chasing more current demand.
**Rule:** Do not confuse a full calendar with durable demand.
**Why it is useful:** Shows why a full calendar can hide a weak future-demand system.

### 03. Booked is not arrived
**Category:** Conversion
**Hook:** Consult volume looks healthy. Show rate is sliding. Where do you intervene?
**Operator call:** Increase commitment after booking — before the patient arrives.
**Rule:** Fix the stage where the behavior actually breaks.
**Why it is useful:** A pre-visit continuity case: healthy booking volume, weak show behavior.

### 04. Warm audience or cold acquisition?
**Category:** Reactivation
**Hook:** You need 30 additional visits next month. Where would you look first?
**Operator call:** Start with people who already have context and trust.
**Rule:** Inspect warm intent before paying for cold attention.
**Why it is useful:** A strong “where would you look first?” case using dormant patient equity.

### 05. Consults happen. Treatments do not.
**Category:** Conversion
**Hook:** Patients are showing up for consults — but too few start treatment. What is your first move?
**Operator call:** Fix the decision environment before adding more consults.
**Rule:** More conversations do not solve a weak decision experience.
**Why it is useful:** Separates consult volume from consult effectiveness.

### 06. New device, wrong audience
**Category:** Patient Growth
**Hook:** A clinic launches a new device. Who should hear about it first?
**Operator call:** Start with the patients whose existing history makes the treatment relevant.
**Rule:** Relevance beats reach when trust already exists.
**Why it is useful:** A cross-sell case that starts with the existing patient base instead of broad promotion.

### 07. The clinic looks full — but is it?
**Category:** Capacity
**Hook:** Overall utilization is 82%. One provider is booked out three weeks. Do you add capacity?
**Operator call:** Fix the demand distribution before expanding fixed capacity.
**Rule:** Do not solve a distribution problem with expansion.
**Why it is useful:** A capacity-allocation problem hidden by an aggregate utilization number.

### 08. The promo filled the calendar — sort of
**Category:** Acquisition
**Hook:** A promotion doubles consult bookings. No-shows also double. Was the campaign successful?
**Operator call:** Do not scale booked appointments that are not turning into patient behavior.
**Rule:** Measure the behavior you actually need — not the step before it.
**Why it is useful:** Tests lead quality and commitment after a high-volume promotion.

### 09. The package sold. The journey stalled.
**Category:** Retention
**Hook:** Patients buy a four-session series, but completion drops after session two. What do you fix first?
**Operator call:** Treat the space between sessions as part of the treatment experience.
**Rule:** A treatment plan is only valuable if the patient stays inside it.
**Why it is useful:** A series-completion case around multi-session treatments.

### 10. Membership signups are fine. Month four is not.
**Category:** Retention
**Hook:** Membership acquisition is healthy, but cancellations spike in month four. What do you investigate first?
**Operator call:** Make the value legible before adding more ways to buy it.
**Rule:** Retention follows perceived value, not purchase intent.
**Why it is useful:** A recurring-revenue case focused on perceived value between visits.

### 11. Happy patients. Quiet reputation.
**Category:** Advocacy
**Hook:** Patient satisfaction is high, but review volume is flat. What is missing?
**Operator call:** Turn satisfaction into a designed moment — not a hope.
**Rule:** Goodwill is an asset only when the system gives it somewhere to go.
**Why it is useful:** Separates satisfaction from an actual review/referral system.

### 12. Five minutes vs fifty
**Category:** Conversion
**Hook:** Lead volume is steady. Conversion fell after the front desk workflow changed. What would you inspect first?
**Operator call:** Restore clear ownership of the first response.
**Rule:** When the system changed, inspect the system before blaming demand.
**Why it is useful:** A response-time case where the operator move is operational, not creative.

### 13. First visit wins. Second visit disappears.
**Category:** Retention
**Hook:** New-patient satisfaction is strong, but too few return within 90 days. Where do you focus?
**Operator call:** Make the second visit part of the first visit.
**Rule:** A great first visit is not a retention strategy.
**Why it is useful:** A new-patient retention case focused on the transition from first experience to ongoing relationship.

### 14. A waitlist with empty chairs
**Category:** Capacity
**Hook:** The clinic has a two-week waitlist — and still finishes the week with empty slots. What is the first move?
**Operator call:** Recover fragmented capacity before creating more capacity.
**Rule:** Unused inventory and a waitlist can exist at the same time.
**Why it is useful:** Shows how scheduling friction can coexist with unused capacity.

## Patient Journey Replay

13 prepared journeys. Each includes a complete before/after timeline, break diagnosis, replay language, patient-facing phone content, and operator takeaway.

### 01. Treatment → Rebook
**Category:** Retention
**Hook:** A treatment can go perfectly and still lose the next visit.
**Break:** The visit succeeded. The continuity system did not.
**Takeaway:** Retention starts before the patient realizes they are deciding whether to return.
**Why it is useful:** The core continuity story: a good treatment does not automatically create the next visit.

### 02. Lead → Consult
**Category:** Acquisition
**Hook:** The lead did not disappear. The momentum did.
**Break:** The clinic paid for attention — then left it unattended.
**Takeaway:** Lead generation creates attention. The follow-up system decides whether attention becomes an appointment.
**Why it is useful:** Shows how paid attention is lost between inquiry and an actual appointment.

### 03. Booking → Arrival
**Category:** Conversion
**Hook:** Booking the appointment is not the same as earning the arrival.
**Break:** The calendar held the appointment. The journey did not hold the patient.
**Takeaway:** Show rate is partly built in the days between booking and arrival.
**Why it is useful:** A no-show prevention story focused on the waiting period after booking.

### 04. Treatment → Aftercare
**Category:** Patient Experience
**Hook:** The appointment ended. The patient experience should not.
**Break:** The moment questions appear is often the moment support disappears.
**Takeaway:** Patient education is not paperwork. It is part of the retention experience.
**Why it is useful:** Turns aftercare from handoff paperwork into part of the actual patient experience.

### 05. Consult → Treatment Decision
**Category:** Conversion
**Hook:** The consult happened. The patient still left without a decision.
**Break:** The consult ended before the decision process did.
**Takeaway:** A consult is a moment. The treatment decision can be a journey.
**Why it is useful:** A decision-confidence story for clinics with healthy consult volume but weak starts.

### 06. Treatment Series → Completion
**Category:** Retention
**Hook:** Selling the series is not the same as completing the series.
**Break:** The gap between sessions became the weakest part of the treatment plan.
**Takeaway:** Treatment adherence lives between appointments as much as inside them.
**Why it is useful:** For multi-session care where adherence drops between visits.

### 07. First Visit → Second Visit
**Category:** Retention
**Hook:** A great first visit can still become a one-time visit.
**Break:** The clinic won the experience — but never designed the continuation.
**Takeaway:** A great first visit is only the beginning of a retention system.
**Why it is useful:** A new-patient retention story: the experience is good, but the relationship never becomes ongoing.

### 08. Lapsed → Reactivated
**Category:** Reactivation
**Hook:** A past patient is not the same thing as a cold lead.
**Break:** Trust existed. Relevance disappeared.
**Takeaway:** Reactivation is often a continuity problem before it is an acquisition problem.
**Why it is useful:** Reactivation through relevance rather than generic “we miss you” outreach.

### 09. No-show → Recovered
**Category:** Recovery
**Hook:** A no-show is a broken appointment — not always a lost patient.
**Break:** The recovery attempt treated the missed appointment like an administrative error.
**Takeaway:** A missed appointment should trigger a recovery journey, not an automatic obituary.
**Why it is useful:** A recovery journey that avoids treating every missed appointment as a dead lead.

### 10. Treatment A → Relevant Treatment B
**Category:** Patient Growth
**Hook:** Cross-sell works better when it feels like the next chapter — not another offer.
**Break:** The clinic knew the patient — but marketed like it did not.
**Takeaway:** Cross-sell becomes patient growth when the recommendation has context.
**Why it is useful:** A cross-sell story based on patient goals and treatment adjacency rather than generic promotions.

### 11. Membership → Continued Value
**Category:** Retention
**Hook:** A membership can be valuable and still feel invisible.
**Break:** The membership kept billing. The value stopped being visible.
**Takeaway:** Membership retention depends on experienced value, not just promised value.
**Why it is useful:** A membership-retention story focused on visibility of benefits between visits.

### 12. Great Result → Referral
**Category:** Advocacy
**Hook:** A happy patient is not automatically a referral source.
**Break:** The clinic earned advocacy — but gave it nowhere to go.
**Takeaway:** Word of mouth grows faster when goodwill has a designed path.
**Why it is useful:** Shows how patient enthusiasm needs a designed path to become advocacy.

### 13. Great Experience → Review
**Category:** Advocacy
**Hook:** Patients can love the clinic and still never leave a review.
**Break:** The review request arrived after the reason to write it had faded.
**Takeaway:** Reviews are an operations system built on timing, context, and low friction.
**Why it is useful:** A reputation journey where timing and friction matter more than generic reminders.
